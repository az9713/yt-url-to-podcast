- Embedding Gemma 2 is a strong open multimodal retrieval model that can be fine-tuned on your own data, improving performance on specific documents, product names, or sounds relevant to your application.
- Demo: Fine-tuned Gemma 2 on transcripts from the creator's YouTube channel; it returns exact video moments with clickable timestamps in response to questions.
- Question addressed: Can a few minutes of fine-tuning noticeably improve the model on your own data without harming existing capabilities?
- Difference from LLM fine-tuning: Embedding models output vectors (embeddings) rather than text, so training targets are not exact outputs but relationships—pairs of inputs that should be close in embedding space.
- Training data consists of pairs (e.g., customer question and relevant passage) without needing labeled wrong answers.
- During training, a batch of pairs is used; for each question, its paired passage is the positive example, other passages in the batch serve as negative examples automatically.
- This approach is known as multiple negatives ranking loss (used in sentence transformers and CLIP).
- Issue: If duplicate passages appear in the same batch (e.g., two customers ask about the same thing), the model mistakenly treats a correct passage as wrong. The fix is a "no duplicate sampler" that prevents duplicate texts in one batch.
- Prompt format matters: Embedding Gemma 2 expects a short task prompt before the search query and documents in title and text format; training and production prompts must match exactly.
- Fine-tuning uses LoRA adapters—small trainable matrices added to frozen model weights—making training fast and requiring saving only a small adapter file (~2% of weights).
- Gemma 2 uses a shared backbone for text, images, video, and audio; fine-tuning on one modality affects others.
- Evaluation: Training loss alone is insufficient; test on unseen questions split by source (e.g., different articles or videos) to avoid overlap and overfitting.
- Example 1: Fine-tuning on ESC-50 dataset (50 everyday sounds, e.g., dog barking, chainsaw).
  - Training pairs: label sentence ("sound of a chainsaw") and audio clip.
  - Without fine-tuning, accuracy was ~25%; after 7.5 minutes of LoRA training on A100 GPU, accuracy on unseen clips rose to ~66%.
  - Some sounds (e.g., brushing teeth) improved to near-perfect identification.
- The model learns labels it is trained on, not general hearing ability; sounds not in training data did not improve.
- Impact on other modalities:
  - Photo and video search performance unchanged.
  - Journal text search dropped by about 4 points, showing some cost to shared backbone fine-tuning.
- Issue found: When loading adapters, audio processor sometimes incorrectly truncated clips, causing performance drop; fixed by reusing base model processor.
- Example 2: Fine-tuning on 120 YouTube video transcripts.
  - Created question-passage pairs using Gemini 3.8 to generate viewer-style questions.
  - Split data by video to ensure test questions were unseen.
  - Before fine-tuning, correct passages returned first ~66% of the time; after under 3 minutes of training, improved to ~75%.
  - General text search again dropped by ~4 points.
- Summary:
  - Few minutes of fine-tuning improves performance on specific sounds and video transcripts.
  - Model learns labels, not general skills; unseen labels do not improve.
  - Fine-tuning slightly reduces general text search quality due to shared backbone.
- Recommendations for fine-tuning Gemini 2:
  1. Prepare data as pairs and avoid duplicates in batches.
  2. Test on unseen data split by source.
  3. Monitor changes in other modalities after fine-tuning.
